When I started my internship at Formulatrix, I was excited—and a little overwhelmed. I was introduced to the Software Engineering team and to the software behind Rock Imager, a system used in protein crystallization. At first, I was mostly learning: setting up my development environment, exploring the codebase, and trying to understand how the software connected to the instruments it helped operate.

Then came my first bug. I traced the problem, found its cause, and worked through a fix. It took a few rounds of testing before everything behaved as expected, but I eventually opened my first merge request. Seeing my work reviewed, approved, and merged made the whole process feel real. Soon, I was fixing more bugs and building a feature that involved both the user interface and the backend. I learned that finishing the code was only part of the job: feedback, testing, and small improvements to the user experience mattered just as much.

As the internship continued, the work became more challenging. I helped update an older frontend, worked through compatibility issues, and tested changes carefully to make sure they didn’t disrupt existing features. I also explored automated testing and wrote unit tests. At first, I focused too much on how many tests I could add. My lead helped me see that good tests should protect what matters most—not just increase a coverage number. That changed how I approached the code: I slowed down, learned the purpose of each component, and checked whether my tests would actually catch a problem.

Some of my most memorable learning came from seeing how software met the physical instrument. I learned about the different parts of Rock Imager and how their states affected one another. One task involved handling what should happen when a connection between systems was lost while the instrument was working. It took discussion, careful testing, and a few simpler rethinks before the solution was right. That experience taught me not to fall in love with my first idea—and to value clear, maintainable fixes over complicated ones.

The internship wasn’t only about code. I joined team meetings, learned from senior engineers, contributed to documentation, and got to know people across the team. I also enjoyed the small moments: team-building activities, futsal after work, and being the office muadzin. Those moments made the workplace feel welcoming, even on the days when a bug was hard to reproduce or a test needed to be rewritten.

Near the end, I had the chance to apply for a junior engineering role. I didn’t get the position, and that was disappointing. But it didn’t erase what the internship had given me: more confidence, a better understanding of software engineering, and a clearer sense of the kind of work I want to explore—especially work that connects software with hardware.

On my last day, I finished one final task and said goodbye to the people who had made the experience special. I left grateful for the opportunity, proud of how much I had learned, and hopeful that our paths might cross again. My internship at Formulatrix wasn’t just a chance to write code. It was a chance to grow, contribute, and discover what I want to learn next.
